abstract: An important psychological phenomenon (or bias) is the Better-than-average effect, whereby people consider themselves to be superior to others in many dimensions that are positive and less likely to engage in negative behaviors. We study the role of this bias in the context of redistribution and paternalistic policies. First, we document that people perceive themselves to be superior to others not only in the standard dimensions explored so far but also along many dimensions that are directly relevant to redistribution policy, such as how they spend their money or government transfers, how they react to policy changes, or the extent to which they free-ride on government policies. Second, we highlight that this bias is especially strong when people compare themselves to lower-income others. The prevalence of this bias differs by education, income, political leaning, and gender. Third, we show that those who have stronger biases support less redistribution and more paternalistic ways of redistributing. This link appears for in a correlational sense and also causally, through our experiment. Our experiment is a de-biasing intervention that helps people understand that they might be prone to such a bias. It comes in three forms, each creating the link to redistribution and paternalistic policies more and more explicitly.

summary: Landlords and tenants, on average, have opposite characteristics; but they display positive assortative matching within rental markets. In a nationwide data set containing administrative information on linked renter-occupiers and owners of investment properties in France, we document assortative matching by income level and composition, wealth, age, marital status and family structure, both across and within fine geographic segments. Consistent with a novel theory of rental housing assignment, the income correlation is only partially explained by observable characteristics such as location, size, or investment timing. This pattern has substantial implications for returns to wealth, and the incidence of housing market policies.
